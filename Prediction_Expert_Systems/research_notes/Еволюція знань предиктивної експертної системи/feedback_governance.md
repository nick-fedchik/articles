# Feedback effects and governed change management for evolving predictive expert systems

Scope: (a) how a deployed predictor's own recommendations distort the data it later learns from, and how to evaluate/learn correctly despite this; (b) binding law, guidance, standards and engineering practice for controlled updates of deployed AI/ML or rule-based safety-relevant systems. Research cut-off: 3 November 2026.

Evidence labels used below: **[BINDING]** = statute/regulation text; **[GUIDANCE]** = official non-binding regulator guidance/principles; **[STD-SCOPE]** = official public abstract/scope of a paywalled standard (no clause text verified); **[RESEARCH]** = peer-reviewed/primary research result (as stated in the abstract unless noted); **[PRACTICE]** = engineering practice documented by practitioners/vendors.

Verification method: DOIs checked via Crossref API; arXiv metadata/abstracts via arXiv API; PubMed abstracts via NCBI E-utilities; FDA guidance PDF read directly; EU legal texts read from the official Publications Office (OJ XHTML) because EUR-Lex returned a bot challenge to automated requests; ISO/IEC pages fetched from iso.org / webstore.iec.ch.

## 1. Feedback loops: performative prediction, self-defeating predictions, informative censoring, and off-policy evaluation

### Takeaway
When predictions trigger interventions, the logged outcome data are no longer samples of the "untreated" process: effective interventions make the model *appear* to degrade and naive refitting can learn the wrong association. Correct evaluation and learning require treating deployment as a logging policy — recording the action taken and its propensity, keeping some randomisation/positivity, and using importance-weighted or doubly robust estimators (or modelling outcome and intervention jointly) instead of plain retraining.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH] Performative prediction (definition).** "When predictions support decisions they may influence the outcome they aim to predict. We call such predictions performative; the prediction influences the target." When ignored, "performativity surfaces as undesirable distribution shift, routinely addressed with retraining." — Perdomo, Zrnic, Mendler-Dünner, Hardt, *Performative Prediction*, ICML 2020, PMLR 119:7599–7609 — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v119/perdomo20a.html); [arXiv:2002.06673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06673)
- **[RESEARCH] Performative stability.** The paper introduces "performative stability", which "implies that the predictions are calibrated not against past outcomes, but against the future outcomes that manifest from acting on the prediction"; main results are "necessary and sufficient conditions for the convergence of retraining to a performatively stable point of nearly minimal loss"; performative prediction "strictly subsumes" strategic classification. — [arXiv:2002.06673](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.06673)
- **[RESEARCH] Self-defeating ("victims of their own success") predictions.** "As users respond to model predictions, downstream characteristics of the data, including the distribution of the outcome, may change… The more effective a model and intervention(s) are at improving outcomes, the faster a model will appear to degrade. Improving outcomes can disrupt the association between the model's predictors and the outcome. Model refitting may not always be the most effective response." — Lenert, Matheny, Walsh, *Prognostic models will be victims of their own success, unless…*, JAMIA 26(12):1645–1650, 2019, DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocz145 (Crossref + PubMed verified) — [PubMed 31504588](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31504588/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocz145)
- **[RESEARCH] Proposed mitigation in the same paper:** problems "will need to be mitigated by systematically incorporating interventions into prognostic models and by maintaining robust performance surveillance of models in clinical use. Holistically modeling the outcome and intervention(s) can lead to resilience to future compromises in performance." — [PubMed 31504588](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31504588/)
- **[RESEARCH, metadata only]** A follow-up commentary argues that "explicit causal reasoning is needed to prevent prognostic models being victims of their own success" — Sperrin, Jenkins, Martin, Peek, JAMIA 26(12):1675–1676, 2019, DOI 10.1093/jamia/ocz197. Only title/bibliographic metadata verified (no abstract available). — [PubMed 31722385](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31722385/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/jamia/ocz197)
- **[RESEARCH] Hidden feedback loops as ML technical debt.** ML-specific risk factors include "boundary erosion, entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers, data dependencies, configuration issues, changes in the external world". — Sculley et al., *Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems*, NeurIPS 2015 — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2015/hash/86df7dcfd896fcaf2674f757a2463eba-Abstract.html)
- **[BINDING] EU AI Act explicitly names feedback loops.** "High-risk AI systems that continue to learn after being placed on the market or put into service shall be developed in such a way as to eliminate or reduce as far as possible the risk of possibly biased outputs influencing input for future operations (feedback loops), and as to ensure that any such feedback loops are duly addressed with appropriate mitigation measures." — Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 15(4) — [OJ text via Publications Office](http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32024R1689); [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)
- **[RESEARCH] Core difficulty of learning from logged decisions.** In contextual bandits, "a central task is evaluation of a new policy given historic data consisting of contexts, actions and received rewards. The key challenge is that the past data typically does not faithfully represent proportions of actions taken by a new policy. Previous approaches rely either on models of rewards or models of the past policy. The former are plagued by a large bias whereas the latter have a large variance." — Dudík, Langford, Li, *Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Learning*, ICML 2011 — [arXiv:1103.4601](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.4601)
- **[RESEARCH] Doubly robust (DR) estimator guarantee.** DR "yields accurate value estimates when we have either a good (but not necessarily consistent) model of rewards or a good (but not necessarily consistent) model of past policy"; empirically it "uniformly improves over existing techniques, achieving both lower variance in value estimation and better policies." — [arXiv:1103.4601](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.4601). Extended journal version: Dudík, Erhan, Langford, Li, *Doubly Robust Policy Evaluation and Optimization*, Statistical Science 29(4):485–511, 2014, DOI 10.1214/14-STS500 (Crossref verified) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1214/14-STS500)
- **[RESEARCH] Counterfactual Risk Minimization (CRM).** Batch learning from logged bandit feedback: the counterfactual nature of the problem is addressed "through propensity scoring"; generalisation bounds "account for the variance of the propensity-weighted empirical risk estimator" and give rise to the CRM principle and the POEM algorithm. — Swaminathan & Joachims, *Counterfactual Risk Minimization: Learning from Logged Bandit Feedback*, ICML 2015, PMLR 37:814–823 — [PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v37/swaminathan15.html); [arXiv:1502.02362](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02362). (A short WWW 2015 companion paper "Counterfactual Risk Minimization", DOI 10.1145/2740908.2742564, pp. 939–941, also exists — Crossref verified.)
- **[RESEARCH] Offline replay evaluation.** Offline evaluation of contextual-bandit policies "is critical for protecting online user experiences but very challenging due to their 'partial-label' nature"; simulator-based evaluation introduces modelling bias; the proposed data-driven *replay* method "can provide provably unbiased evaluations" (Yahoo! Front Page news data). — Li, Chu, Langford, Wang, WSDM 2011 — [arXiv:1003.5956](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.5956)
- **[RESEARCH] Causal inference for changes to deployed learning systems.** Causal inference can be leveraged "to understand the behavior of complex learning systems interacting with their environment and predict the consequences of changes to the system", illustrated on Bing ad placement. — Bottou et al., *Counterfactual Reasoning and Learning Systems* — [arXiv:1209.2355](https://arxiv.org/abs/1209.2355) (journal venue not re-verified in this session)

### Inferences
- **Self-defeating predictions in preventive maintenance (inference by analogy; no PdM-specific primary source verified).** The Lenert et al. mechanism maps directly onto predictive maintenance: a high RUL-risk prediction triggers replacement/servicing, which right-censors the failure that would have occurred. The censoring is *informative* (it depends on the model's own risk score), so standard survival-analysis assumptions of non-informative censoring are violated, and the logged data under-represent failures precisely in the high-risk region. Naive retraining can then learn that "high-risk" features are associated with fewer failures — the "appears to degrade" effect.
- **What the logs must contain for valid counterfactual evaluation** (follows from the estimators above): for every decision, the context/features as seen at decision time, the action actually taken (e.g. "intervene now", "defer", "inspect"), the probability with which the logging policy chose that action (propensity), the model/rule-base version that produced it, and the eventual outcome (including censoring time and reason). Dudík et al. explicitly define the data as "contexts, actions and received rewards"; CRM and IPS/DR additionally need propensities.
- **Positivity / exploration (standard property of importance weighting, stated here as inference).** Inverse-propensity weights have the form π_new(a|x)/π_log(a|x); if the logging policy is deterministic (π_log(a|x)=0 for actions the new policy would take), these weights are undefined and the effect of the alternative action is not identified from data. Only a reward-model ("direct method") extrapolation remains, which Dudík et al. characterise as "plagued by a large bias". Hence a deployed safety-relevant system that wants to learn must either (i) keep a small, safety-bounded randomised share of decisions (e.g. randomised inspection deferral within a safe margin), (ii) exploit documented natural variation (capacity limits, scheduling), or (iii) accept that evaluation of new policies rests on unverifiable modelling assumptions.
- **High variance is the price of correction.** CRM's variance-aware bounds and DR's variance reduction exist because IPS with small propensities has large variance; in safety settings this argues for logging propensities that are bounded away from zero (clipping/min-exploration rate) and for reporting confidence intervals, not point estimates, at change-approval gates.
- **Performative stability as a release criterion.** Perdomo et al. suggest evaluating a model against outcomes generated *under its own deployment*; for a governed system, this suggests a post-deployment check that repeated retraining converges (model deltas shrink) rather than oscillates — an oscillating retrain loop is a signal of strong performative effects requiring causal modelling instead of more retraining.
- **Regulatory linkage.** Art. 15(4) of the EU AI Act makes "feedback loops" a design obligation for high-risk systems that continue learning, so the mitigations above (intervention-aware modelling, propensity logging, surveillance) can be cited as technical means of compliance — this is an engineering interpretation, not an official one.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed source specifically on intervention-induced (self-defeating) censoring in *industrial* predictive maintenance was found in the bounded search (arXiv query returned survival-model papers handling ordinary right censoring only). The PdM mapping above is analogical.
- The precise convergence conditions in Perdomo et al. (sensitivity/smoothness/strong-convexity assumptions) were not re-verified from the full text; only the abstract-level claims are reported.
- The explicit positivity statement and the uniform-random-logging assumption of Li et al.'s replay method were not verified from the full papers in this session (abstracts only).
- Sperrin et al. (2019) content not verified beyond title/metadata.

## 2. Regulatory and standards framework for updating learning systems (status as of November 2026)

### Takeaway
Two binding regimes now explicitly accommodate pre-planned evolution: US FD&C Act §515C (Predetermined Change Control Plans, with FDA's final AI-specific PCCP guidance, current version 18 Aug 2025) and the EU AI Act (pre-determined changes of continuously-learning systems are not a "substantial modification" if assessed at the initial conformity assessment and documented in Annex IV point 2(f)). The EU high-risk obligations (incl. Art. 12 logging and Art. 15(4) feedback loops) were postponed by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 to 2 Dec 2027 (Annex III) and 2 Aug 2028 (Annex I). Functional-safety standards (IEC 61508, ISO 26262-8, ISO 21448) and AI management standards (ISO/IEC 42001, 5338) require managed change/lifecycle processes, but their clause texts are paywalled and only their public scopes were verified.

### Inferences
- **The two regimes converge on the same pattern:** pre-authorise a *bounded envelope* of changes (FDA: Description of Modifications; EU: Annex IV 2(f) pre-determined changes), a *method* for making them (FDA: Modification Protocol; EU: "technical solutions adopted to ensure continuous compliance"), and *evidence* that they stay within the envelope (FDA: Impact Assessment + monitoring; EU: Art. 72 PMM and Art. 12 logs). A change outside the envelope triggers a new authorisation (FDA: new 510(k)/PMA supplement; EU: new conformity assessment under Art. 43(4)).
- **For a rule-based/neuro-symbolic expert system**, "changes to the AI system and its performance that have been pre-determined" can include knowledge-base rule additions and threshold re-calibrations, provided their admissible range and validation method are specified up front. Adding a new intended purpose or changing software architecture would, per Art. 3(23) and recital 128, likely be substantial. This is an interpretation, not legal advice.
- **Art. 12 is change-aware.** Its purpose (a) explicitly links logs to *detecting situations that may result in a substantial modification*, i.e. the logging design must make silent drift-induced or learning-induced changes in behaviour visible.
- **Application nuance (flag for legal review):** Art. 12 and Art. 15 are in Chapter III Section 2, so they apply from 2 Dec 2027 / 2 Aug 2028 per amended Art. 113. Art. 72 sits in Chapter IX (general date 2 Aug 2026), but it concerns high-risk systems whose core obligations are deferred, and its template guidance is now due 2 Sep 2027. The precise interplay was not confirmed from an official interpretation.
- **Status flags.** FDA AI-PCCP guidance: final, current version 18 Aug 2025 (what changed from Dec 2024 was not determined). General PCCP guidance: still draft as far as could be determined. GMLP: 2021 principles superseded/extended at IMDRF level (Jan 2025). ## 3. Engineering practice for safe updates

### Takeaway
Practice converges on staged, reversible, measured release of model/knowledge changes — offline counterfactual evaluation, then shadow or canary exposure to a small slice with automated comparison against the incumbent, then promotion with a retained pointer to the previous version for rollback — backed by a test-and-monitoring rubric (ML Test Score) and an assurance argument that is updated with each change (dynamic safety cases). Regulators echo this: the PCCP principles require "mechanisms to detect and revert or stop implementation of a change".

### Cited Findings
- **[PRACTICE] Canarying (Google SRE Workbook, ch. "Canarying Releases").** "We define canarying as a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation. This evaluation helps us decide whether or not to proceed with the rollout. The part of the service that receives the change is 'the canary,' and the remainder of the service is 'the control.'" The canary normally covers "a much smaller subset of production"; "Canarying is effectively an A/B testing process." Release-engineering principles listed include reproducible builds, automated builds and tests; "Smaller, self-contained release artifacts make it cheaper and easier to roll back any given release artifact in the event of a bug." — [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
- **[PRACTICE] ML-specific release.** "Production deployment of a new version of an ML model usually goes through A/B testing or online experiments before the model is promoted to serve all the prediction request traffic"; deployment teams use "security, regression, and load and canary testing"; "Model monitoring: The model predictive performance is monitored to potentially invoke a new iteration in the ML process." — Google Cloud Architecture Center, *MLOps: Continuous delivery and automation pipelines in machine learning* — [Google Cloud](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/mlops-continuous-delivery-and-automation-pipelines-in-machine-learning)
- **[PRACTICE] Rollback via metadata.** The ML metadata store records "A pointer to the previous trained model if you need to roll back to a previous model version or if you need to produce evaluation metrics for a previous model version". — [Google Cloud MLOps](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/mlops-continuous-delivery-and-automation-pipelines-in-machine-learning)
- **[RESEARCH/PRACTICE] ML Test Score.** Breck, Cai, Nielsen, Salib, Sculley, *The ML test score: A rubric for ML production readiness and technical debt reduction*, 2017 IEEE International Conference on Big Data, pp. 1123–1132, DOI 10.1109/BigData.2017.8258038 (Crossref verified). Presents "28 specific tests and monitoring needs, drawn from experience with a wide range of production ML systems", noting it is hard to formulate tests "given that the actual prediction behavior of any given model is difficult to specify a priori". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/BigData.2017.8258038); [Google Research abstract](https://research.google/pubs/the-ml-test-score-a-rubric-for-ml-production-readiness-and-technical-debt-reduction/)
- **[RESEARCH] Hidden technical debt.** Quick ML "wins" are not free; "it is common to incur massive ongoing maintenance costs in real-world ML systems", with risk factors including entanglement, hidden feedback loops, undeclared consumers, data dependencies, configuration issues and changes in the external world. — Sculley et al., NeurIPS 2015 — [NeurIPS proceedings](https://papers.nips.cc/paper_files/paper/2015/hash/86df7dcfd896fcaf2674f757a2463eba-Abstract.html)
- **[RESEARCH] Dynamic safety cases.** Denney, Pai, Habli, *Dynamic Safety Cases for Through-Life Safety Assurance*, ICSE 2015 (NIER), pp. 587–590, DOI 10.1109/ICSE.2015.199 (Crossref verified): "dynamic safety cases, a novel operationalization of the concept of through-life safety assurance, whose goal is to enable proactive safety management"; motivated with an aviation example; defines principles, a lifecycle, and "key elements required to move towards a formalization of the associated framework". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE.2015.199); [NASA NTRS 20150011054](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20150011054)
- **[GUIDANCE] Revert/stop mechanism.** PCCP principle "Focused and Bounded" includes "mechanisms to detect and revert or stop implementation of a change that fails to meet specified performance criteria". — [FDA PCCP principles](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices/predetermined-change-control-plans-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices-guiding-principles)
- **[RESEARCH] Offline evaluation as a safety gate.** Offline evaluation is "critical for protecting online user experiences" (Li et al. 2011) — [arXiv:1003.5956](https://arxiv.org/abs/1003.5956)

### Inferences
- **Recommended staged pipeline for a predictive expert system (synthesis):** (1) offline regression tests on sequestered data (FDA) + ML Test Score checks; (2) counterfactual/OPE estimate of the new policy's value from propensity-logged history (DR estimator, with confidence intervals); (3) *shadow* mode — the candidate computes recommendations on live inputs without acting, enabling comparison of predictions/disagreement rates without performative effects; (4) *canary* — the candidate acts on a small, time-limited slice with automated comparison to control; (5) promotion; (6) post-market monitoring; with (7) a one-step rollback to the retained previous version at every stage.
- **Shadow mode cannot measure outcome effects of actions**, because shadow recommendations are not executed — it measures prediction agreement and input-distribution behaviour only. Outcome-level comparison requires canary/A-B exposure or OPE, which in turn requires the positivity/propensity logging of Section 1. The feedback-loop problem therefore makes the canary slice also a source of exploration data.
- **Champion/challenger** is the same structure as control/canary with an explicit incumbent ("champion") retained until the challenger demonstrably dominates on pre-registered metrics; the PCCP "Evidence-based" principle (benefits and risks "before and after" the change) maps onto pre-registered champion-vs-challenger acceptance criteria.
- **Dynamic safety cases** provide the assurance-side analogue of continuous delivery: each change should trigger re-evaluation of the GSN argument nodes whose evidence it invalidates (e.g. a retrained model invalidates test-evidence nodes; a new rule invalidates hazard-coverage nodes), rather than a full re-certification.

### Gaps
- No authoritative primary source (peer-reviewed or standards) defining **shadow deployment** or **champion/challenger** was verified in this session; the descriptions above are synthesised engineering practice.
- Specific quantitative canary parameters (slice size, duration, statistical tests) were not extracted from the SRE Workbook beyond the definition.
- Later work on dynamic/through-life assurance of ML components (e.g. AMLAS, UL 4600) was outside the bounded scope and not verified.

## 4. Evidence to record per change to make evolution auditable

### Takeaway
Auditable evolution requires a per-change record that ties *what changed* (versioned artefacts with provenance) to *why* (rationale, pre-authorised envelope), *how it was validated* (data, metrics, results, before/after comparison), *how it was released and could be reverted*, and *what was observed afterwards* (logs and monitoring) — plus decision-level logs with propensities so that the change's effects can later be evaluated counterfactually.

### Cited Findings
- **[GUIDANCE]** FDA expects a PCCP with title and version number, referenced in the authorisation letter; each modification described (Description of Modifications), traceable to its verification/validation method (Modification Protocol §VII.C), with an Impact Assessment of benefits and risks; data management practices (incl. sequestered test data), re-training practices, performance evaluation, update procedures (labeling, user transparency, post-market surveillance, real-world monitoring). — [FDA PCCP guidance PDF](https://www.fda.gov/media/166704/download)
- **[BINDING]** EU technical documentation must describe pre-determined changes and the technical solutions ensuring continuous compliance (Annex IV 2(f)), the validation and testing procedures, data used and their characteristics, and metrics (Annex IV 2(g)), and the post-market evaluation system/PMM plan (Annex IV 9). — [Reg. 2024/1689](http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32024R1689)
- **[BINDING]** Automatic event logs over the system lifetime, sufficient to identify risk situations or substantial modifications and to support post-market monitoring (Art. 12(1)–(2)). — [Reg. 2024/1689](http://publications.europa.eu/resource/celex/32024R1689)
- **[GUIDANCE]** PCCP principles call for characterisation of "the device before and after implementation of changes", of the data used in modifications, and management of "individual and cumulative changes". — [FDA PCCP principles](https://www.fda.gov/medical-devices/artificial-intelligence-enabled-medical-devices/predetermined-change-control-plans-machine-learning-enabled-medical-devices-guiding-principles)
- **[PRACTICE]** For each ML pipeline execution, record "the pipeline and component versions that were executed", start/end times and duration, "the executor of the pipeline", "the parameter arguments", pointers to produced artefacts (prepared data, validation anomalies, computed statistics, vocabularies), and a pointer to the previous trained model for rollback/comparison. — [Google Cloud MLOps](https://cloud.google.com/architecture/mlops-continuous-delivery-and-automation-pipelines-in-machine-learning)
- **[RESEARCH]** Counterfactual evaluation of any future change needs historic "contexts, actions and received rewards" (Dudík et al.) and propensities (Swaminathan & Joachims). — [arXiv:1103.4601](https://arxiv.org/abs/1103.4601); [arXiv:1502.02362](https://arxiv.org/abs/1502.02362)
- **[STD-SCOPE]** ISO 26262-8 places configuration management, change management, verification and documentation management among required supporting processes. — [ISO 26262-8 page](https://www.iso.org/standard/68390.html)

### Inferences
- **Minimal per-change evidence record (synthesis of the above; not a quoted requirement):**
  1. *Identity & versioning:* change ID; semantic version of model/rule base/thresholds/feature code/config; hash of every artefact; previous version pointer.
  2. *Provenance:* training/knowledge sources with dataset versions and date ranges, labelling/reference-standard method, pipeline/component versions, executor, parameters.
  3. *Rationale & envelope:* triggering evidence (drift alarm, incident, new requirement); whether the change is inside the pre-authorised envelope (PCCP / Annex IV 2(f)) or requires new authorisation/conformity assessment; impact/hazard analysis delta.
  4. *Validation:* sequestered test-set identity, metrics with confidence intervals before vs after, subgroup results, OPE/DR estimates for decision-policy changes, ML Test Score items passed.
  5. *Release record:* shadow/canary configuration, slice, duration, acceptance criteria and observed results, approver(s), timestamp, rollback plan and test.
  6. *Assurance update:* list of safety-case (GSN) nodes invalidated and re-substantiated.
  7. *Operational logs after release:* per-decision record of inputs, model/rule version, output, action taken, propensity, human override, and outcome/censoring — enabling Art. 12-style traceability and later counterfactual evaluation.
- **Cumulative-change accounting** (PCCP "individual and cumulative changes") implies keeping a lineage graph, not just the latest diff, so that drift of the system relative to the originally assessed baseline can be measured.

### Gaps
- No single official checklist of per-change evidence fields was found; the list above is a synthesis. ISO/IEC 42001 and 5338 likely contain relevant documented-information requirements, but clause-level text was not accessible.
- Retention periods for logs (e.g. AI Act Art. 19/26 provisions) were not researched in this session.
