# Temporal logic, interval calculi, and runtime verification for predictive expert systems

**Evidence cutoff:** 2 November 2026. **Scope:** publicly accessible papers, preprints, project documentation, and dataset documentation. This is a targeted evidence map, not a systematic review; claims below are classified as peer-reviewed results, preprint claims, or research hypotheses. No book chapter was edited.

## What established work supports

### Takeaway

Signal Temporal Logic (STL) and Metric Temporal Logic (MTL) provide a precise way to express time-bounded requirements over signals or timed events. Runtime monitors can evaluate a trace against those requirements and, in quantitative STL, return a robustness margin. Recent work extends the question from “does the observed trace satisfy the formula?” to “given partial observations, sensor uncertainty, and a learned forecast, what can be guaranteed about future satisfaction?” The strongest demonstrated predictive integration is robust conformal predictive runtime verification (RPRV), but its probabilistic guarantees are conditional on explicit distributional assumptions and have so far been demonstrated mainly in simulated CPS, rather than industrial degradation or expert-system deployments.

### Cited findings

- **Quantitative STL semantics are a trace-level margin, not a probability of prediction correctness.** Donzé and Maler define robust satisfaction for real-valued signals: the sign of robustness corresponds to satisfaction/violation, and its magnitude measures distance to a boundary under the chosen semantics. This provides a useful margin for thresholding and optimization, but it does not by itself quantify sensor confidence, forecast calibration, or probability of future failure. [Donzé & Maler (2010), “Robust Satisfaction of Temporal Logic over Real-Valued Signals,” FORMATS 2010, LNCS 6418, pp. 92–106, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15297-9_9).
- **MTL monitoring can provide proof-carrying explanations of Boolean verdicts.** Lima, Herasimau, Raszyk, Traytel, and Yuan construct an online MTL monitor whose verdicts include proof trees explaining why a policy was satisfied or violated. They give a sound and complete proof system and a formally verified checker in Isabelle. This establishes a route to inspectable MTL evidence; it does not explain the origin or calibration of a learned prediction. [Lima et al. (2023), “Explainable Online Monitoring of Metric Temporal Logic,” in *Runtime Verification*, pp. 473–491, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-30820-8_28).
- **MTL, STL, and timed-event monitoring are related but not interchangeable.** MTL supplies metric constraints over timed propositions; STL is used for real-valued signal predicates and quantitative robustness. A 2026 Reelay preprint reports one implementation framework spanning LTL, MTL, STL, discrete-/dense-time semantics, and robustness, but its performance and scope claims remain preprint claims as of the cutoff. [Alur & Henzinger (1993), “Real-Time Logics: Complexity and Expressiveness,” *Information and Computation* 104(1), 35–77, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1006/inco.1993.1025); [Ulus (2026), “Reelay: Online Temporal Logic Monitoring Framework,” arXiv:2604.22384](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22384).
- **Partial-trace monitoring can preserve “not enough evidence” rather than forcing a Boolean answer.** Deshmukh et al. formalize online robust monitoring of partial traces and report automotive and CPS-education case studies; the paper reports savings in expensive simulation that outweighed monitoring overhead. The accessible version consulted is an arXiv manuscript; its peer-reviewed publication status was not verified here, so its empirical claims are not treated as an independent peer-reviewed result. [Deshmukh et al. (2015), “Robust Online Monitoring of Signal Temporal Logic,” arXiv:1506.08234](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.08234).
- **Uncertain observations can be treated as part of the semantics rather than silently replaced by point values.** Finkbeiner, Fränzle, Kohn, and Kröger study interval-bounded per-sample error with an unknown fixed offset plus independently varying error. They give model-free and linear-model-based evaluation algorithms using affine arithmetic and SMT over linear arithmetic. Their result is that temporal reasoning can sometimes yield a determinate verdict even when evaluating Boolean predicates over individually possible state estimates would remain inconclusive. The guarantee is tied to their specified uncertainty/error models; it is not a general solution for arbitrary missingness, sensor faults, or learned-forecast error. [Finkbeiner et al. (2022), “A Truly Robust Signal Temporal Logic: Monitoring Safety Properties of Interacting Cyber-Physical Systems under Uncertain Observation,” *Algorithms* 15(4), 126](https://doi.org/10.3390/a15040126).
- **Runtime monitoring can report how observations cause a verdict, not only the current robustness.** An et al. introduce Boolean and quantitative causation monitors for STL, derive classic monitors from the proposed framework, and report more detailed evolution information without a significant increase in monitoring cost. The full version states that it was accepted by CAV 2023; the linked public record is the arXiv manuscript. This addresses explanation of observed-trace verdicts, not causal explanation of a model’s forecast or a guarantee about real future events. [An et al. (2023), “Online Causation Monitoring of Signal Temporal Logic,” arXiv:2305.17754](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.17754).
- **Predictive runtime verification has a statistically explicit treatment of forecast uncertainty and distribution shift.** Zhao et al. propose RPRV for stochastic CPS and multi-agent systems. A learned model predicts system trajectories; robust conformal prediction is then applied to STL/STREL robustness to produce probabilistic guarantees. Their analysis assumes a known upper bound on a statistical distance (an f-divergence) between deployment and design-time trajectory distributions. Experiments use a drone-swarm simulator and examine scalability and the effects of different trajectory predictors. This is a peer-reviewed journal article, but its results do not establish the same guarantee for an uncharacterized shift or for industrial degradation trajectories. [Zhao, Zhu, Hoxha, Fainekos, Deshmukh & Lindemann (2025), “Distributionally Robust Predictive Runtime Verification under Spatio-Temporal Logic Specifications,” *ACM Transactions on Cyber-Physical Systems* 9(4), 1–26, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/3748818); [open manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.02964).
- **Related model-predictive monitoring exists, but is not synonymous with learned prediction.** Yu, Dong, Li, and Yin publish a method on model-predictive monitoring of dynamical systems against STL requirements. It is evidence that prediction/lookahead and monitoring can be coupled; the bibliographic record alone does not establish a calibrated learned predictor, statistical coverage under shift, or an application to prognostics. [Yu et al. (2024), “Model predictive monitoring of dynamical systems for signal temporal logic specifications,” *Automatica* 160, 111445, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.automatica.2023.111445).
- **Qualitative interval reasoning complements, but does not replace, signal monitoring.** Allen’s interval algebra represents temporal relations between intervals (for example, before, overlaps, during, and meets), useful for reasoning about phases or events such as “incipient degradation precedes severe degradation.” Krokhin, Jeavons, and Jonsson prove that satisfiability over the full Allen algebra is NP-complete and classify exactly 18 maximal tractable subalgebras. This is a concrete design constraint: an expert system that combines many unconstrained qualitative interval relations may lose tractability, while a chosen tractable fragment can support efficient reasoning. These results concern qualitative interval constraints, not STL’s quantitative robustness or predictive calibration. [Allen (1983), “Maintaining Knowledge about Temporal Intervals,” *Communications of the ACM* 26(11), 832–843, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/182.358434); [Krokhin, Jeavons & Jonsson (2003), “Reasoning about Temporal Relations,” *Journal of the ACM* 50(5), 591–640, DOI](https://doi.org/10.1145/876638.876639).
- **Tools expose different semantics and timing assumptions, so implementation configuration is part of the claim.** RTAMT documents offline and online STL monitoring for discrete- and dense-time interpretations, with online monitors for bounded-future STL; its documentation also describes periodic-sampling interpretations and timing assumptions. A monitor verdict is therefore conditional on the selected time model, sampling interpretation, signal representation, and formula—not an assumption-free property of raw sensor data. [RTAMT project documentation](https://github.com/nickovic/rtamt).
- **Recent performance claims should be separated from established results.** The 2026 `mstlo` manuscript describes an incremental Rust/Python STL monitor, RoSI and Boolean early verdicts, per-operator caching, and streaming temporal extrema. Its authors report benchmark improvements for deep/nested formulas, but this is an arXiv preprint as of the cutoff, not a peer-reviewed result in the consulted source. It is a useful candidate implementation for reproducible comparisons, not evidence that monitoring cost is solved. [Kaag Thomsen et al. (2026), “mstlo: Efficient Online Monitoring of Signal Temporal Logic,” arXiv:2605.26847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26847).
- **Survey claims are kept separate from algorithmic results.** Bartocci, Mateis, Nesterini, and Nickovic survey methods for mining STL specifications. That survey is useful for organizing specification-mining approaches and applications; it is not evidence that a learned degradation predictor has been validated by a runtime monitor. The empirical and formal claims in this note are attributed to the primary papers above, not inferred from the survey. [Bartocci et al. (2022), “Survey on mining signal temporal logic specifications,” *Information and Computation* 289, 104957](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ic.2022.104957).

### Inferences

- For a predictive expert system, temporal logic can serve as a **decision-evidence layer** above a forecast: encode admissible degradation trajectories or maintenance deadlines, evaluate predicted traces and uncertainty sets, then map guaranteed satisfaction to an accepted recommendation, a boundary-crossing or inconclusive result to a qualified recommendation, and a guaranteed violation to a refusal/escalation. That three-way action policy is a proposed system design, not an outcome established by the cited papers.
- Robustness, forecast confidence, and epistemic uncertainty are distinct quantities. A positive STL robustness margin means the represented trace satisfies the formula with a margin; it must not be presented as a probability that the real system will remain safe. A conformal coverage statement has a probability interpretation, but only under the calibration and shift assumptions of its method.
- The literature reviewed here demonstrates temporal monitoring and prediction-to-monitor integration in CPS. It does **not** establish a complete pipeline for an evidence-governed predictive expert system that combines degradation forecasts, sensor-data uncertainty, temporal event relations, and auditable accept/qualify/reject decisions.

### Gaps

- The evidence base is asymmetric: formal monitoring methods are well developed for CPS signals and safety specifications, while direct evaluation on public run-to-failure prognostics datasets and predictive expert systems is not demonstrated in the cited predictive-RV work.
- Predictive-monitor papers differ in what is predicted (system trajectory, controller/model behavior, or a scalar remaining-useful-life estimate). A scalar RUL forecast cannot directly certify a trajectory property unless assumptions translate it into a crossing-time interval or a set of possible future signals.
- No surveyed claim here establishes universal soundness or completeness across STL/MTL variants. Those properties must be stated relative to the monitor’s formal semantics, signal interpolation, timestamp model, and uncertainty assumptions.

## Unresolved technical issues at the temporal/prediction boundary

### Takeaway

The central unresolved issue is not the absence of temporal operators; it is how to give a truthful verdict when the trace itself is uncertain. Noise, missing samples, timestamp uncertainty, interpolation, and predictive-model error all change the set of possible traces. If the monitor collapses that set to one estimated trace, a crisp answer may be computationally convenient but unjustified. If it preserves uncertainty, more outputs may be inconclusive and the expert system must know how to act on them.

### Cited findings

- **Noise and sensor bias:** The 2022 uncertain-observation paper directly studies bounded per-sample error and an unknown fixed offset, and shows that temporal aggregation can provide information not available from independently evaluated state estimates. Its formal guarantees rely on the stated error structure and bounds; broader stochastic, heteroscedastic, correlated, or adversarial sensor errors require additional models. [Finkbeiner et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3390/a15040126).
- **Missing observations:** Partial-trace semantics can return an unresolved result when the observed prefix does not determine truth. This is preferable to treating imputed values as facts, but a missing interval with no physical or statistical bound leaves an effectively larger set of possible traces. Any definite verdict then depends on additional assumptions (such as bounded rate of change, a state model, or a missingness mechanism). [Deshmukh et al. (2015), preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.08234); [RTAMT documentation](https://github.com/nickovic/rtamt).
- **Timestamp uncertainty and granularity:** A timestamped irregular stream may be interpreted as discrete samples, a dense-time signal reconstructed between samples, or a periodically sampled signal with jitter. These are different semantics. RTAMT documents discrete-/dense-time monitoring and timing assumptions; the reviewed predictive-RV results do not establish a general timestamp-uncertainty calculus that simultaneously accounts for jitter, out-of-order delivery, clock drift, and missing samples. [RTAMT documentation](https://github.com/nickovic/rtamt).
- **Monitor complexity:** Real-time cost depends on formula structure, bounded temporal windows, trace length, nesting, and the chosen time representation; implementation-specific optimizations do not remove the need to report these dimensions. In a separate qualitative formalism, unrestricted Allen-algebra satisfiability is NP-complete while 18 maximal fragments are tractable, illustrating that expressiveness can create a sharp complexity trade-off. [Krokhin et al. (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1145/876638.876639); [RTAMT](https://github.com/nickovic/rtamt); [`mstlo` preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.26847).
- **Soundness and completeness:** A monitor can be sound with respect to a mathematical trace abstraction yet unsound with respect to the physical signal if its interpolation or error envelope excludes reality. Conversely, conservative bounds may be sound but inconclusive often. RPRV adds a probabilistic layer and explicitly assumes an upper bound on distribution shift; it does not turn a trajectory predictor into a deterministic physical guarantee. [Finkbeiner et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3390/a15040126); [Zhao et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3748818).
- **Temporal representation mismatch:** Allen-style interval relations can express phase ordering and overlap; STL/MTL can express signal thresholds and bounded response times. A hybrid system needs a defined mapping from noisy signal segments to event intervals and from those intervals to quantitative rules. The two formalisms’ semantics and complexity results do not automatically compose. [Allen (1983)](https://doi.org/10.1145/182.358434); [Krokhin et al. (2003)](https://doi.org/10.1145/876638.876639); [Donzé & Maler (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15297-9_9).

### Inferences

- For every experiment and deployment, define a **trace contract**: units, clock source, permitted timestamp error, sampling mode, interpolation model, bounds on missing spans, sensor-error envelope, and whether the specification uses future or past time. Without this contract, reported precision/soundness cannot be reproduced or compared.
- A robust monitor should return a set or interval of possible robustness values when evidence is incomplete. Under the conventional sign interpretation, a lower robustness bound above zero supports guaranteed satisfaction within the modeled trace set; an upper bound below zero supports guaranteed violation; an interval crossing zero should remain explicitly inconclusive. This is a proposed operational rule whose adequacy must be validated for the chosen monitor and application.
- A useful predictive expert system should preserve separate fields for (i) forecast distribution/coverage, (ii) temporal robustness bounds, and (iii) expert-rule decision status. Combining them into one “confidence” score would hide which evidence actually supports the recommendation.
- The key comparison is not just monitor execution time. It is the trade-off among false-safe verdicts, false alarms, inconclusive/abstention rate, decision lead time, and compute/memory cost.

### Gaps

- Public run-to-failure data usually provide sampled signals and labels, not ground-truth continuous trajectories between samples or calibrated timestamp-error bounds. A benchmark can therefore validate claims about sampled-trace semantics and controlled perturbations, but not silently treat reconstructed continuous paths as ground truth.
- Shift-aware conformal guarantees need calibration examples and a justified bound on deployment shift. In practice, both may be unavailable or estimated from the same limited data; sensitivity to misspecified shift bounds is an important unresolved evaluation dimension.
- The reviewed work offers no generally accepted benchmark protocol for comparing monitor soundness, completeness, and predictive calibration under the same noise/missingness/time-jitter conditions.
- A universal completeness objective is inappropriate when the evidence is insufficient. Research should report when the monitor abstains and how its abstention policy affects downstream decisions, rather than optimizing Boolean coverage alone.

## Falsifiable research proposals and benchmarks

### Takeaway

Three experiments can test the most actionable gaps without claiming novelty in advance: bound-aware monitoring under synthetic sensor corruption; conformal temporal checking of degradation forecasts under condition shift; and soundness/latency analysis under timestamp jitter and changing temporal granularity. Each proposal includes a falsification criterion so that null results are informative.

### Cited findings

- NASA’s Prognostics Data Repository provides public run-to-failure time-series data for prognostic-algorithm development. The repository description notes trajectories from nominal behavior toward failure; C-MAPSS is a commonly used turbofan degradation benchmark within this collection. Dataset documentation and the particular subset/version should be cited in any experiment. [NASA Prognostics Data Repository](https://www.nasa.gov/intelligent-systems-division/discovery-and-systems-health/pcoe/pcoe-data-set-repository/).
- The temporal-logic methods above supply candidate monitor families: quantitative STL robustness, partial-trace/RoSI-style monitoring, uncertain-observation evaluation, and predictive runtime verification with robust conformal prediction. These are methods to compare, not interchangeable assumptions. [Donzé & Maler (2010)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-642-15297-9_9); [Finkbeiner et al. (2022)](https://doi.org/10.3390/a15040126); [Zhao et al. (2025)](https://doi.org/10.1145/3748818).

### Inferences

#### Proposal 1 — Sensor uncertainty and missing spans

- **Question / hypothesis:** Does monitoring a set of possible signals reduce false-safe prognostic decisions versus point imputation at a matched decision-coverage level? Hypothesis: with correctly specified measurement-error and missing-span bounds, a conservative monitor will not issue a definite satisfaction verdict for any sampled trace whose oracle trace violates the rule, while returning more inconclusive decisions than point imputation.
- **Data and method:** Use NASA C-MAPSS subsets with engine-level train/calibration/test splits. Start from complete observed windows, inject independently controlled missing blocks, point dropouts, bounded additive noise, and fixed sensor offsets; use the uncorrupted sampled sequence as oracle for **sampled-time** semantics only. Include a separate analytic synthetic degradation generator with known continuous ground truth to test dense-time behavior. Compare (a) linear/forward imputation plus ordinary STL, (b) a monitor using interval-bounded observations, and (c) a simple partial-trace monitor. Example rules: “always below a temperature limit until the forecast horizon” and “eventually cross a degradation threshold within the next H cycles.”
- **Measures:** False-safe and false-alarm rates; proportion of oracle robustness values contained by the monitor’s reported interval; inconclusive fraction; time-to-first-definite-verdict; CPU time and peak memory. Stratify by missing-span length, error bound, sensor, and temporal-window width.
- **Falsification:** The soundness claim is falsified by any definite “satisfied” verdict when the oracle trace violates the formula under the declared bounds. A claimed practical benefit is falsified if, at matched decision coverage, interval monitoring does not lower false-safe risk relative to point imputation, or if its compute cost exceeds a preregistered operational limit. Do not interpret injected missingness as evidence about naturally occurring missing-data mechanisms.

#### Proposal 2 — Forecast uncertainty, temporal constraints, and operating-condition shift

- **Question / hypothesis:** Does a calibrated distribution over future degradation trajectories, evaluated by a temporal monitor, improve risk-controlled recommendation quality over point RUL predictions? Hypothesis: under the tested calibration/shift assumptions, a conformal or otherwise coverage-calibrated trajectory set will lower false-safe decisions, at the cost of more qualified/abstaining outcomes.
- **Data and method:** Train trajectory or quantile predictors on C-MAPSS training engines; use engine-separated calibration and test splits, and evaluate across operating-condition/fault subsets (FD001–FD004) as a stress test rather than assuming these subsets alone establish a formal shift bound. Add a synthetic controlled-shift experiment where the deployment perturbation and its bound are known. Check each future trace set against STL rules such as “remain below a critical vibration/temperature limit through the next service interval,” “cross a degradation threshold within H cycles,” and “degradation rate remains below a limit.” Compare a point-prediction monitor, calibrated prediction intervals without temporal logic, and a distributionally robust predictive monitor. Include a scalar-RUL baseline only when its crossing-time assumptions are made explicit.
- **Measures:** Empirical coverage of future trajectory sets and robustness intervals; false-safe/false-alarm risk; acceptance/qualification/refusal or abstention rates; lead time before threshold crossing; calibration error under each shift; prediction error (for example, MAE) as a secondary metric; monitoring latency.
- **Falsification:** The guarantee claim is falsified if the observed coverage falls below its preregistered nominal level beyond a stated statistical tolerance under the assumptions claimed by the method. The system-value hypothesis is falsified if, at matched false-safe risk, the temporal monitor does not improve lead time or reduce unnecessary refusals versus the calibrated-forecast-only baseline. Report failure when the shift bound is misspecified rather than excluding that condition.

#### Proposal 3 — Timestamp jitter, sampling granularity, and interval-event reasoning

- **Question / hypothesis:** Can a monitor preserve sound verdicts as irregular sensor timestamps and temporal granularity vary, while retaining useful decision coverage? Hypothesis: representing each event time as a bounded interval and monitoring a set of consistent traces avoids definite verdict flips caused only by bounded timestamp jitter, although it may increase inconclusive outcomes.
- **Data and method:** Generate analytic degradation trajectories with known threshold-crossing times and known phase intervals (normal, incipient, severe). Create timestamp jitter with known bounds, variable-rate sampling, downsampling, delayed/out-of-order observations, and bounded missing spans. Compare (a) a discrete-time monitor after resampling, (b) a dense-time monitor with a fixed interpolation assumption, and (c) a monitor that reasons over bounded timestamp intervals. Add Allen relations for phase ordering/overlap, restricted to a selected tractable subalgebra; separately stress unrestricted relation sets to expose combinatorial cost. Use NASA C-MAPSS sampled trajectories as a secondary empirical check, but do not claim their timestamps reveal continuous-time truth.
- **Measures:** Definite-verdict soundness against the synthetic oracle; verdict agreement across sampling rates; rate of correct/inconclusive/incorrect verdicts; latency from observation to verdict; memory and CPU versus horizon, formula depth, and interval-relation count.
- **Falsification:** Any definite incorrect verdict under the stated jitter and interpolation bounds falsifies the soundness hypothesis. The claimed robustness advantage is falsified if verdict-flip frequency is not lower than the resampled baseline at comparable decision coverage. The tractability claim is falsified if the selected Allen fragment exhibits superlinear or otherwise budget-violating behavior at the preregistered relation sizes.

### Gaps

- C-MAPSS supports reproducible degradation experiments but does not supply continuous-time ground truth, real clock drift, or every kind of missingness; synthetic truth is necessary for the strongest soundness tests.
- None of the proposals presumes that an experimental result will be novel. First compare against the primary works and tools cited above, publish formulas, perturbation generators, split definitions, package versions, and all inconclusive cases, then assess novelty.
- For operational use, define acceptable risk thresholds with domain stakeholders before experiments. Statistical significance alone does not establish that a false-safe rate, abstention rate, or monitoring latency is acceptable in a safety-relevant decision process.
